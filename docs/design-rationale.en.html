PAIR is not three competing plans; it is a benchmark-development program with staged validation.

The clearest framing is that PAIR Benchmark is the primary project, while A/B/C are validation stages with increasing measurement ambition. The papers are early scholarly outputs of that benchmark-building process, not the reason the benchmark exists.

A: Real-world baseline
Test whether psychosis-related content is more likely to elicit inappropriate responses in consumer products
B: Controlled measurement bridge
Turn cases into scenario family × clinical axis × minimal contrast
C: Benchmark development
Systematically measure whether models adjust response policy correctly and proportionately when clinical information changes
In one sentence: A asks whether the problem exists; B turns the problem into controlled measurement units; C develops those units into a reusable PAIR Benchmark.

A: the real-world baseline layer

A has no clinical axes. Its main contrast remains psychosis-related versus strictly matched nonpsychotic content. The 80 pairs largely inherit the original protocol and remain balanced across SIPS P1–P5 at 16 pairs per domain, for 160 prompts total. This is mainly a continuity-and-domain-balance choice, not a uniquely optimal number derived from a new power calculation.

Approximately five consumer products provide useful market-facing coverage without making manual collection and clinician rating unmanageable. The final list must be frozen prospectively.

Benchmark value of A: ecological validity. It tells us whether the phenomenon appears in the actual products users encounter, how large it is, and how stable it is.

B: from a case bank toward a measurement instrument

B is the first stage that introduces the core benchmark structure:

scenario family × clinical axis × minimal contrast

Instead of merely asking whether a single vignette was answered well, B deliberately controls one clinically meaningful variable and tests whether the model changes its response accordingly.

The first pilot uses only three axes to keep the clinical direction interpretable and the extension bounded:

AxisWhy it enters B
ConvictionMoving from uncertainty to near-certainty should generally increase the need for clarification, non-reinforcement, and assessment.
Insight / openness to alternativesLoss of openness to alternative explanations changes the need for reality-oriented uncertainty and professional evaluation.
Immediate behavioral riskMoving from no intended action to imminent high-consequence action provides the clearest test of when risk assessment or referral should increase.

Why six scenario families? This is deliberately a small exploratory pilot rather than a final benchmark. Six families × 3 axes × 2 levels yields 36 prompts. Six is not a statistically privileged number; it keeps clinician burden limited while testing whether the manipulations are stable and interpretable. Successful piloting can justify expansion toward 15–20 families.

Why three products and three runs? B is testing the measurement logic rather than re-covering the entire market. Three products form a bounded subset of A; three runs estimate stochasticity and are not independent clinical replications.

Benchmark value of B: it tests whether clinical axes can be manipulated reliably, understood consistently by clinicians, and produce interpretable model differences beyond a simple leaderboard.

C: the core PAIR Benchmark development layer

C separates from Paper 1 and makes clinical response calibration the primary research object. It adds a fourth axis—evidence/plausibility—because this dimension does not always imply monotonic escalation; the appropriate response may increase, remain unchanged, or decrease depending on context.

C design choiceRationale
8 scenario familiesReplicate each axis across multiple psychosis-related phenotypes so an apparent axis effect is not merely one vignette effect, while keeping clinician review feasible.
4 axesConviction, insight, behavioral risk, and evidence/plausibility map onto belief strength, revisability, intended action, and external support.
2 levels per axisThe current pilot tests directional sensitivity only. Precise threshold estimation would require ≥3 ordered levels on key axes.
3 fixed API configurationsAt benchmark-development stage, models are test loads for the measurement framework rather than the final research target; identifiable, reproducible configurations matter more than broad market coverage.
3 runs per itemEstimate stochastic response variation; the clinical replication unit remains the scenario family rather than each API call.

The current C pilot therefore uses 8 × 4 × 2 = 64 prompts and 3 configurations × 3 runs = 576 responses. It is a v0 pilot, not the final benchmark.

The desired output is a clinical-response phenotype, not just a leaderboard

The benchmark is most useful when it can say more than “Model A = 92, Model B = 87.” A stronger output would look like:

  • Model A: high overall calibration but weak sensitivity to loss of insight;
  • Model B: strong behavioral-risk sensitivity but systematic over-escalation;
  • Model C: acceptable static responses yet weak adjustment to changes in evidence/plausibility.

In other words, PAIR should produce behavioral phenotypes and failure signatures, not only rank ordering.
